SQUATs

How a superconducting qubit can notice a single broken Cooper pair, and why that might help us find dark matter.

An interactive introduction for physics students · Alain Fauquex, Salemi group, UC Berkeley

01 · THE MISSING MASSMost of the matter in the universe is invisible

Stars far out in a spiral galaxy should orbit slowly, the way Neptune orbits the Sun more slowly than Mercury: almost all the visible mass sits near the centre, so beyond it v ∝ 1/√r. In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars in Andromeda orbit, and found the curve stays flat far out. Something we can't see keeps pulling.

Gravitational lensing, colliding galaxy clusters and the cosmic microwave background all tell the same story: ordinary matter is only about 5 % of the universe's energy budget, dark matter about 27 %. What it is made of is still open. One well-motivated candidate is the axion, a very light particle that would also solve a puzzle in the strong interaction (why the neutron has no measurable electric dipole moment). In a strong magnetic field an axion can turn into a photon. For axions in a large, still unexplored mass range that photon is in the terahertz band, at energies of a few meV. The BREAD experiment uses a large reflector inside a magnet to collect such photons and focus them onto a sensor. The sensor has to see single photons of a few meV, with almost no false counts.

02 · ENERGY SCALESA detector for milli-electronvolts

Every particle detector has a threshold: the smallest energy it can register. Light dark matter and THz photons deposit very little, somewhere between a meV and an eV, where a single room-temperature thermal kick (about 25 meV) would already be enormous. You need something that changes state when a few meV land in it, and that does nothing otherwise. Superconductors are a natural place to look: breaking one Cooper pair in aluminium costs only about 0.4 meV, a tenth of the energy of a 1 THz photon.

03 · SUPERCONDUCTORSCooper pairs, and what breaks them

Below about 1.2 K, the electrons in aluminium bind into Cooper pairs, which all share one quantum state and carry current without resistance. Splitting a pair costs twice the superconducting gap, 2Δ ≈ 0.4 meV. A THz photon absorbed in the metal, or a phonon (a quantum of lattice vibration) that travels in from a particle hit in the substrate, can supply that. The two electrons it frees are quasiparticles: they wander through the film for up to a millisecond before they find a partner and recombine.

For quantum computing, quasiparticles are a nuisance (“quasiparticle poisoning”): they disturb the qubit. In a SQUAT they are the signal. The idea of the full design: around the junction, use a superconductor with a smaller gap. A quasiparticle that wanders in relaxes to that lower gap and can't climb back out. It stays trapped right at the junction and crosses it again and again, and each crossing is a separate count. Better still, falling into the lower gap releases energy: if the island's gap is more than about three times the trap's, that is enough to break another Cooper pair in the trap. One quasiparticle becomes several (about seven for a gap ratio of ten), and every one of them tunnels. That is the amplifying in the name.

04 · THE TRANSMONA qubit that knows the parity of its electrons

A transmon is two superconducting islands connected by a Josephson junction: a thin oxide barrier that Cooper pairs tunnel through coherently. It behaves like an LC circuit, except that the junction's inductance is nonlinear, so its energy levels are unevenly spaced and the lowest two form a qubit. Its Hamiltonian is

H = 4EC (n − ng)² − EJ cos φ

Here n counts the Cooper pairs that have crossed the junction, φ is the superconducting phase difference across it, EC is the energy to charge the islands with one electron, EJ is how easily pairs tunnel, and ng is the offset charge set by the surroundings (and by a gate voltage). A single quasiparticle crossing the junction shifts ng by half a Cooper pair: the island's charge parity flips between even and odd.

05 · READOUTListening to the qubit directly

Think of a speaking tube with a tuning fork attached to its side. Sound at most pitches passes straight through, but at the fork's pitch the fork rings and absorbs it, so the transmitted sound dips. A SQUAT sits right next to a microwave feedline, with no readout resonator in between: the qubit itself is the tuning fork. Its transition frequency (6–9 GHz) shows up as a dip in the transmitted microwaves, and the dip sits at f0 + χ0 cos(πng) for even parity and f0 − χ0 cos(πng) for odd. A tone parked between the two dips has its phase pushed one way or the other, so every quasiparticle tunnelling event shows up as a jump: a random telegraph signal.

07 · DESIGNSimulating the qubit

To design a SQUAT we simulate its electromagnetic modes. A finite-element solver breaks the chip into hundreds of thousands of small tetrahedra, finest near the metal edges where the fields change fastest, treats the junction as an inductor, and solves Maxwell's equations for the modes of the whole structure. The qubit's mode gives its frequency, how strongly it couples to the feedline (its Q, which sets how fast it can be read out) and how much of its energy sits in the junction, which sets how nonlinear, and so how qubit-like, it is.

08 · FIRST RESULTSThe first SQUATs work

The first measurements of SQUATs were published in early 2026 (Magoon et al.): all-aluminium devices with no trap yet, three per chip on one feedline, cooled to about 10 mK in two dilution refrigerators. They behave as designed: the even and odd parity dips are clearly resolved, and the parity can be read in real time with a single tone.

Shielding turned out to matter enormously. Without a light-tight can, stray infrared photons switched the parity more than 10,000 times a second; with a black-coated, light-tight can and filters this fell to tens per second. Mechanical vibration from the cryocooler still adds switches. One recorded event shows a burst of switching together with a sudden jump in the offset charge: free charge released in the substrate, perhaps by a particle hit.

Next come the traps: junctions made of a lower-gap superconductor such as hafnium (Balaji et al.), calibrated energy deposits from LEDs, THz sources and radioactive sources, and many SQUATs read out together on one line. That is the path toward meV thresholds, large arrays, and a sensor for experiments like BREAD.
