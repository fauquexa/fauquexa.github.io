Look through (default)

  1. Go fullscreen, or make the image big.
  2. Put your nose near the screen so everything is blurry, and stare as if looking at something far behind it.
  3. Lean back slowly without refocusing. The two focus dots split into four; when the middle two merge into three, hold still.
  4. The shape surfaces out of the pattern. Once it locks in, you can look around it.

Cross-eyed

  1. Switch "Viewing method" to Cross-eyed.
  2. Hold a fingertip halfway between your eyes and the screen and focus on it.
  3. Move the finger until the focus dots behind it look like three, then shift your attention to the image while keeping that crossing.
How stereograms work

Two eyes, two views

Your eyes sit about 6 cm apart, so each one sees the world from a slightly different spot. Something close lands in quite different places in the two views; something far away barely shifts. Your brain reads that difference, called disparity, as depth.

A pattern that fools them

When you look “through” the screen, as if at something far behind it, your two eyes stop aiming at the same spot and each lands on a different dot. If those two dots look the same, which happens all the time in a repeating pattern, your brain merges them into one point and places it where the two lines of sight meet: behind the screen.

Hiding a shape

For every point of the hidden shape, the generator works out which two pixels your eyes would see it through, and links them: they must get the same colour. Nearer points have their pair closer together, so where the shape is, the pattern repeats a little sooner. Up close it looks like noise; once your eyes fuse it, those small shifts become a floating shape.

How this page builds one

  1. Depth map. The logo or Julia set becomes a grey image, lighter where it should be nearer, softened a little so the pattern bends at the edges instead of tearing.
  2. Linking. Each row is scanned and every pair of pixels that must match is linked, skipping points one eye can’t see because something nearer is in the way. This is the method of Thimbleby, Inglis and Witten, “Displaying 3D images: algorithms for single-image random-dot stereograms”, IEEE Computer 27(10), 1994.
  3. Colours. Each linked group takes its colour from the background pattern, starting at the centre of the picture and working outward, so any leftover distortion spreads thinly to both sides.

Tips for viewing

Make the picture big and give it a minute the first time. Start with your nose close to the screen, then lean back slowly; when the focus dots look like three, hold still. If the shape looks sunken instead of raised, you’re viewing it the other way round: switch the viewing method.
