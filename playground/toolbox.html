Formulas, assumptions and references

Geometry (quasi-static conformal mapping)

k0 = w / (w + 2s),   k1 = sinh(πw/4h) / sinh(π(w + 2s)/4h),   k′ = √(1 − k²)
εeff = 1 + ½(εr − 1) · [K(k1)/K(k1′)] · [K(k0′)/K(k0)]
Z0 = (30π / √εeff) · K(k0′)/K(k0)

K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, computed with the arithmetic–geometric mean, K(k) = π / (2 AGM(1, k′)), to machine precision. The line is a CPW with semi-infinite ground planes on a substrate of thickness h with vacuum below it (no backside metal), quasi-TEM, lossless and non-dispersive. Then C′ = √εeff / (c Z0) and L′ = Z0√εeff / c.

Metal thickness

Δ = (1.25t/π)[1 + ln(4πw/t)],   we = w + Δ,   se = s − Δ
εeff,t = εeff − 0.7(εeff − 1)(t/s) / [K(k0)/K(k0′) + 0.7t/s]

Z0 then uses ke = we/(we + 2se) and εeff,t. Set t = 0 to ignore thickness. For thin films (t ≪ s) the correction is about 1–2 %.

Anisotropic sapphire

A uniaxial substrate with in-plane (across the line) permittivity εx and out-of-plane εy is mapped to an isotropic one with εr = √(εxεy) and thickness h√(εx/εy). The presets use ε⊥ = 9.27 and ε∥ = 11.35 (sapphire near 4 K, Krupka et al.). Editing εr by hand switches to an isotropic custom substrate.

Kinetic inductance

Δ = 1.764 kBTc,   λ = √(ħρn / (πμ0Δ)) / √tanh(Δ/2kBT),   Ls = μ0λ coth(t/λ)

For t ≪ λ this is the familiar Ls = ħRs/(πΔ) with Rs = ρn/t (local, dirty-limit Mattis–Bardeen, valid well below Tc). The kinetic inductance per length is Lk′ = g Ls with the CPW geometric factor g = gc + gg:

gc = [π + ln(4πw/t) − k ln((1+k)/(1−k))] / [4w(1 − k²)K(k)²]
gg = k[π + ln(4π(w+2s)/t) − (1/k) ln((1+k)/(1−k))] / [4w(1 − k²)K(k)²],   k = k0

The total line has L′ = Lg′ + Lk′, Z0 = √(L′/C′), v = 1/√(L′C′) and kinetic fraction α = Lk′/L′. The geometric factor assumes t ≪ w, s.

Formulas, assumptions and references

v = 1/√(L′C′) = (c/√εeff)·√(1 − α),   f0 = v/(4ℓ) for λ/4,   f0 = v/(2ℓ) for λ/2

The kinetic inductance fraction α lowers the frequency by √(1 − α) compared with a perfect conductor. Harmonics are at 3f0, 5f0… (λ/4) and 2f0, 3f0… (λ/2), ignoring dispersion.

Near f0 both resonators behave like a parallel LC circuit with

C = C′ℓ/2,   L = 8L′ℓ/π² (λ/4) or 2L′ℓ/π² (λ/2).

Bare frequency. f0 is the frequency of an isolated resonator with an ideal open (and, for λ/4, an ideal short). The coupling capacitor, the open-end fringing field and the junction at the shorted end all pull it down. With Cc to a low-impedance feedline the estimate is f ≈ f0/√(1 + Cc/C); for a λ/2 resonator coupled at both ends, enter the sum of the two capacitances. Expect a few per cent lower frequencies in practice; simulate the final layout.

  • M. Göppl et al., J. Appl. Phys. 104, 113904 (2008): lumped-element equivalent of a CPW resonator.
  • D. M. Pozar, Microwave Engineering, 4th ed. (Wiley, 2012), ch. 6: transmission-line resonators.
  • J. Gao, PhD thesis, Caltech (2008): kinetic inductance fraction and frequency shift.

Formulas, assumptions and references

Saturation vapour pressure from the Magnus form with the Alduchov–Eskridge constants (pressures in hPa, temperatures in °C):

over water: ew(T) = 6.1094 exp(17.625T / (243.04 + T))   (−40 to 50 °C)
over ice: ei(T) = 6.1121 exp(22.587T / (273.86 + T))   (−80 to 0 °C)

The vapour pressure is e = RH · ew(Troom). The dew point solves ew(Td) = e and the frost point solves ei(Tf) = e:

Td = 243.04 γ / (17.625 − γ),   γ = ln(e/6.1094);   Tf = 273.86 γi / (22.587 − γi),   γi = ln(e/6.1121)

A part condenses water if 0 °C ≤ Tpart < Td and collects frost if Tpart < 0 °C and below the frost point (frost forms before dew when the dew point is below freezing). The recommended temperature is the onset temperature plus your margin. Absolute humidity uses ρv = e/(RvT) with Rv = 461.5 J kg−1 K−1. Check: 20 °C and 50 % give a dew point of 9.3 °C.

In practice: the plate thermometers lag the parts that actually see the air (radiation shields, cold fingers, the sample stage), so give a margin, vent with dry nitrogen or helium gas rather than room air, and keep the cans closed until everything is above the dew point.

  • O. A. Alduchov and R. E. Eskridge, J. Appl. Meteorol. 35, 601 (1996): improved Magnus form approximation of saturation vapor pressure.

Inch threads (UNC and UNF)

Unified inch screw threads from #0-80 to 1 inch, with tap drills for about 75 % thread engagement, clearance holes and the closest ISO metric coarse thread. Search for “4-40”, “1/4”, “UNF” or “M3”.

ThreadSeriesMajor ØTPIPitch Tap drillClose clearanceFree clearanceNearest metric

Notes and references
  • Major diameters and threads per inch follow ASME B1.1. Numbered sizes have major diameter 0.060 in + 0.013 in × N. Pitch = 25.4 mm / TPI.
  • Tap drills give roughly 75 % thread engagement, the usual shop recommendation; for hard materials or deep holes one size larger is common. Number and letter drills are converted to mm exactly from their inch sizes.
  • Close and free clearance holes follow the common shop tables (Machinery’s Handbook style); close is for accurate location, free for easy assembly.
  • The nearest metric thread is the ISO coarse thread (ISO 261/262) with the closest major diameter. It is a size guide only; the threads are not interchangeable.
  • ASME B1.1, Unified Inch Screw Threads (UN, UNR, and UNJ Thread Forms).
  • E. Oberg et al., Machinery’s Handbook (Industrial Press): tap drill and clearance hole tables.
  • ISO 261 and ISO 262, ISO general purpose metric screw threads.
